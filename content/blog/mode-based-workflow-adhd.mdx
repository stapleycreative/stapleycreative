## Phases don't work for my brain

Every design process I was ever taught runs the same way: Discover → Define → Design → Deliver. Or some variant. Squares with arrows. Pipeline.

That has never matched how I actually do good work.

I don't move through phases. I move between **states** — sometimes three at once, sometimes looping back to a state I left an hour ago because a screenshot I took triggered a new association. The pipeline metaphor assumes an orderly forward motion my nervous system does not reliably produce.

I have ADHD. My brain is lateral, associative, easily flooded, and highly sensitive to the pressure of being expected to produce *the right thing* before exploration has happened. When I force myself into phase-based process, two things happen: I freeze at the start (can't begin until the plan is right), or I fake the plan to relieve the anxiety and then do the real work afterward, off-process.

So I stopped fighting the shape of my brain and designed a workflow around it.

## Six modes, one gate

### DUMP — constrained divergence
Force raw volume under time pressure. Don't be good, just don't stop. Fill pages. Star anything with resonance. This mode's job is to flush the obvious so it stops occupying bandwidth.

### COLLECT — passive assembly
Screenshots, art boards, scattered notes. "I like this." "Remember this." The act of collecting *is* the processing — my brain builds an associative library in the background. The mess is functional. Don't clean it up.

### SCOPE — parameter lock
Distinguish load-bearing constraints from convention-masquerading-as-requirement. Three tiers: Fixed (structural), Nominal (industry-convention, challengeable), Open (where freedom actually lives). Once locked, hold the parameters — don't re-derive them mid-build.

### CRAFT — the safe build
Build the conservative, defensible option with obsessive attention to detail. Fonts, spacing, padding. Every decision intentional. Measure against the best in the industry.

This mode does two jobs, both critical:
1. It proves execution capability.
2. **It relieves the psychological pressure of needing something to show.**

That second one is survival-level. Without a safe build in hand, anxiety blocks every other mode. I cannot explore until I have something defensible.

### EXPLORE — liberated creativity
*Prerequisite: the safe build exists.* Now the riskier, more interesting directions become possible. Second, third, fourth options, built fast. These are often the best work — fast because the craft standard is already loaded.

### REFINE — cross-pollination
Discoveries from exploration inform the safe build. Everything converges. The final is stronger than any single option because it's built on the full process.

## The Pressure Relief Gate

<PullQuote>Anxiety blocks creative work. A safe, defensible build in hand turns the anxiety off. Only then does exploration become possible.</PullQuote>

There is exactly one non-negotiable gate in this system, and it sits between Craft and Explore.

The gate is met when:
- A presentable, defensible artifact exists.
- I've acknowledged it as "solid enough to show."
- The craft layer is genuine — not a rough draft labeled "done."

**Before the gate:** I'm in Craft mode. The work is careful, a little painful. I'm building to survive the pressure of having to produce.

**After the gate:** Mental space opens. Exploration becomes possible. The second, third, and fourth options — the ones that are often the best work — can finally emerge.

If I try to skip Craft and go straight to Explore, the anxiety drags me back to Craft mid-exploration and I produce nothing usable in either mode. The gate isn't a bureaucratic step. It's a nervous-system requirement.

## Why neurodivergent design matters even if you're not neurodivergent

This workflow was built for my brain. But the principles generalize.

Every designer has panic days. Deadline weeks. Periods of low executive function from poor sleep or a difficult life event. On those days, phase-based process fails *everyone*. The neurotypical designer just has more days where they can muscle through it.

A mode-based process with explicit pressure-relief built in is a process that works on your worst days. That's the test I care about. Not "does this work when everything is going well" — does this work when everything isn't.

## How I use this with AI

Each mode gets a different kind of AI support. I don't use the same prompt style across the workflow.

- **DUMP:** I ask the AI to match volume, not quality. Generate more. Don't filter. Don't critique. If the model tries to evaluate in this mode, I stop and reset.
- **COLLECT:** I ask for wide, lateral references — "find me things like X" — with light resonance notes, no categorization.
- **SCOPE:** I ask for adversarial challenge on what's actually load-bearing. "Is this truly required, or is it just what everyone does?"
- **CRAFT:** I ask for obsessive detail-level critique. Typography. Spacing. Every micro-decision justified.
- **EXPLORE:** I ask for three strong directions, each with a clear POV. Not fifteen weak ones.
- **REFINE:** I ask for synthesis. "Which elements from which exploration are earning their way into the final?"

The AI is doing completely different jobs in each mode. Treating it as one consistent assistant across the whole workflow would waste it.

## For other neurodivergent designers

If you've ever felt like the standard design process was written for a brain that isn't yours — it was. That doesn't mean you're worse at design. It means the process is a bad fit.

Name your modes. Name your pressure points. Name the one gate that unlocks your best work. Then build the workflow that serves those specifically.
