## The question every design leader is quietly asking

AI tools generate full interfaces from text now. Lovable, v0, Cursor, Magic Patterns. Fast. Getting faster. Reshaping what design means.

So where does the designer fit.

I've used these tools every day for a year. Not as toys. As core materials. Prototypes with Claude, component libraries with v0, interactive artifacts that used to take a team two weeks.

Here's what a year of that showed me. The tools are extraordinary at execution and consistently, almost reliably, terrible at three things.

## Diagnosis

Every project starts with a stated problem. "Form is too slow." "Users aren't converting." "Dashboard is cluttered."

The stated problem is almost never the real one.

At GiveCampus everyone assumed the contact report form needed speed. Fifteen fields. Too much clicking. Fifteen fields, too much clicking, so the obvious fix was to simplify it. But speed was never the problem. The form threw away the meaning of the relationship it was supposed to capture. Gift officers filed reports because compliance made them, not because the tool helped them understand a single donor better.

Seeing that, the real problem sitting under the stated one, changed the whole project. No AI tool surfaces it. Not for lack of intelligence. Diagnosis lives outside the interface: how the org actually works, what the domain rewards, and watching someone struggle to name a thing they can't quite say out loud.

## Behavioral judgment

An AI tool generates a notification system. It can't know the system should be opt-in by default because your users have rejection sensitivity and surprise stimuli trigger fight-or-flight.

When I designed Hiki, a social platform for neurodivergent adults, the standard playbook did real damage. Push notifications, infinite scroll, social proof. Not merely ineffective. Harmful. Infinite scroll isn't a neutral pattern anywhere, but on a user with rejection sensitivity it's a trap with a bow on it. Knowing that takes a model of human behavior the tools don't carry.

This isn't a niche concern. Every interface hits a nervous system. Most designers skip it because the assumed user is neurotypical, emotionally neutral, fully present. That user is a fiction for a large share of the people you're actually designing for. Design for the real range and the product gets better for everyone.

## Architectural taste

At iFIT I had to decide: build shared components across five surfaces, or build a token layer and let each surface compose its own layouts. Both seem reasonable on paper. One compounds consistency over three years. One compounds fragmentation.

Tokens first, components second. For two weeks that decision showed nothing. No UI, nothing to screenshot, nothing to demo. Then every component built after it came out consistent across treadmill, bike, rower, app, and web without anyone forcing it. The other path, components first and tokens extracted later, builds the exact fragmentation the system was supposed to prevent.

AI will build you either one. It can't tell you which to pick. That call comes from watching systems compound or rot over years, learning which decisions echo for three years and which ones nobody ever feels. No shortcut buys you that.

## The designer's real job

Design isn't being replaced by AI. It's being stripped down to what it always was. When execution costs almost nothing, the value moves to the part that was always doing the real work and was always hard to see: diagnosis, judgment, taste.

The designers who thrive won't be the ones resisting the tools, and they won't be the ones who become tool operators either. They'll be the ones using AI to amplify judgment that only comes from years of watching humans and systems collide, and who can say out loud why that judgment matters.

That's the work. The tools changed. The work didn't.
