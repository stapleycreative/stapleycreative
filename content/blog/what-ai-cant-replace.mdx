## The question every design leader is quietly asking

AI tools generate full interfaces from text now. Lovable, v0, Cursor, Magic Patterns. Fast. Getting faster. Reshaping what design means.

So where does the designer fit.

I've used these tools every day for a year. Not as toys. As core materials. Prototypes with Claude. Component libraries with v0. Interactive artifacts that would take a team two weeks.

Here's what I see: tools are extraordinary at execution. They're consistently terrible at three things.

## Diagnosis

Every project starts with a stated problem. "Form is too slow." "Users aren't converting." "Dashboard is cluttered."

The stated problem is almost never the real one.

At GiveCampus everyone assumed the contact report form needed speed. Fifteen fields. Too much clicking. The obvious fix: simplify it. Real problem wasn't speed. The form discarded the meaning of donor relationships. Gift officers filed reports because compliance demanded it, not because the tool helped them understand the relationship.

That reframe. seeing the actual problem underneath. changed everything. No AI tool surfaces that. Not because tools lack intelligence. Diagnosis requires context outside the interface: how the org actually works, domain expertise, watching users struggle with what they can't say out loud.

## Behavioral judgment

An AI tool generates a notification system. It can't know the system should be opt-in by default because your users have rejection sensitivity and surprise stimuli trigger fight-or-flight.

When I designed Hiki. a social platform for neurodivergent adults. the standard playbook (push notifications, infinite scroll, social proof) wasn't just ineffective. It was actively harmful. Infinite scroll isn't a neutral pattern. It's a dopamine trap. Knowing the difference requires a model of human behavior that design tools don't have.

This isn't niche. Every interface hits the nervous system. Most designers don't account for it because the default user is assumed neurotypical, emotionally neutral, cognitively present. That assumption breaks for a lot of your users. Designing for the full range makes products better for everyone.

## Architectural taste

At iFIT I had to decide: build shared components across five surfaces, or build a token layer and let each surface compose its own layouts. Both seem reasonable on paper. One compounds consistency over three years. One compounds fragmentation.

Tokens first, components second. that decision was invisible the first two weeks. No UI. But every component built after was automatically consistent across treadmill, bike, rower, app, web. The other path (build components, extract tokens later) creates exactly the fragmentation the system was designed to prevent.

AI tools build either approach. They can't tell you which to choose. That judgment comes from watching systems succeed and fail over time. Understanding which decisions compound. Which don't matter. There's no shortcut for that kind of taste.

## The designer's real job

Design isn't being replaced by AI. It's being clarified. When execution is cheap, the premium shifts to what was always valuable but hard to see: diagnosis, judgment, taste.

Designers who thrive aren't the ones resisting tools or becoming tool operators. They're the ones using AI to amplify judgment that only comes from years watching humans and systems interact. And who can explain why that judgment matters.

That's the work. Tools changed. The work didn't.
