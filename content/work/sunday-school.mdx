## Why this is on a design portfolio

This isn't a religious project. It's an emotional design project.

The problem I was solving: how do you help someone question something they've built their identity around? Not convince them they're wrong. Not argue with them. Help them sit with dissonance long enough to ask their own questions.

That's a design problem. The content is the medium, but the methodology (how you sequence information, how you manage emotional temperature, how you create space for cognitive dissonance without triggering defensive shutdown) transfers directly to any context where you're designing for belief change, behavior change, or emotionally loaded decisions.

Onboarding flows that overcome skepticism. Health products that help people face difficult information. Financial tools that surface uncomfortable truths about spending. Any product where the user needs to change how they think, not just what they click.

## The methodology

Religious belief is a load-bearing structure. It holds up identity, community, family relationships, moral framework, afterlife expectations, and daily routine. You can't just remove it. The person needs to build something else before they can let go. Otherwise the whole structure collapses, and the brain knows this. That's why evidence alone doesn't work. The brain rejects evidence that threatens load-bearing beliefs faster than it processes the evidence itself.

So I designed around this.

### Step 1: Enter through the existing framework

Every piece of content started inside the believer's own worldview. Not challenging it. Using it. Referencing the same texts, the same language, the same values. The person needed to feel understood before they could feel questioned.

This is the same principle behind good onboarding: you meet the user where they are, not where you want them to be. You use their language, their mental model, their existing context.

### Step 2: Introduce contradictions the framework can't resolve

Once inside the framework, I introduced specific, concrete contradictions from within the source material itself. Not external critiques. Internal inconsistencies. Things the believer's own tradition says that contradict other things the tradition says.

The key was specificity. Vague challenges bounce off. Specific, sourced, internal contradictions create genuine cognitive dissonance because the person can't dismiss them as outsider ignorance.

### Step 3: Create space, not conclusions

The content never told anyone what to think. It presented the contradiction and stopped. The design of the space around the content (visual tone, comment moderation, community norms) was built to hold dissonance without resolving it. People needed time to sit with the question before they could find their own answer.

Most persuasion design makes the mistake of closing too fast. Presenting evidence and immediately asking for conversion. That triggers the exact defensive response you're trying to avoid. Leaving space is counterintuitive but it's the only thing that works with load-bearing beliefs.

## The emotional design layer

Beyond the content methodology, the platform design itself was an emotional design exercise. Every visual decision had an emotional intent.

**Typography** was warm and bookish, not clinical or aggressive. The content dealt with serious intellectual material and the type needed to signal "we're thinking together" not "I'm proving you wrong."

**Color** was neutral and warm. No red (aggression), no cold blue (authority), no black (opposition). The palette said "safe space for difficult questions."

**Comment design** prioritized depth over volume. Threaded conversations, not drive-by reactions. The community norms were baked into the interaction design: you could respond thoughtfully or not at all.

**Pacing** was intentional. Content released slowly. No firehose of challenges. Each piece had time to breathe, to be discussed, to be absorbed before the next one arrived.

## The transfer

The methodology I built here shows up in everything I design now. Not the religious content. The emotional architecture.

When I designed Hiki's onboarding for neurodivergent users, I was using the same principle: enter through the user's existing framework (their experience of other dating apps) before introducing a different model (scroll-based profiles, consent-first interactions). When I designed iFIT's profile hub, the challenge was similar: users had a belief about what a profile should be (static information display) that needed to shift toward something new (dynamic progress tracking). The methodology scales.

Any product that asks users to change how they think (not just what they click) benefits from this approach. Meet them where they are. Introduce dissonance from within their own framework. Create space for them to find their own conclusion. Don't close too fast.

That's what this project taught me. And it's not something you learn from a design systems blog post.
