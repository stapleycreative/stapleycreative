## Why this is on a design portfolio

This isn't a religious project. It's an emotional design project.

The problem I was solving: how do you help someone question something they've built their identity around? Not convince them they're wrong. Not argue with them. Help them sit with dissonance long enough to ask their own questions.

That's a design problem. The content is the medium, but the methodology (how you sequence information, how you manage emotional temperature, how you create space for cognitive dissonance without triggering defensive shutdown) transfers directly to any context where you're designing for belief change, behavior change, or emotionally loaded decisions.

Onboarding flows that overcome skepticism. Health products that help people face difficult information. Financial tools that surface uncomfortable truths about spending. Any product where the user needs to change how they think, not just what they click.

## The platform

The website (missedinsunday.com) and social media presence distilled complex religious doctrine into sourced, sharable content. Every piece followed the same format: a direct quote from an official church source, properly cited, designed as a sharable card. The tagline I kept coming back to was simple: "How can you break through a belief?"

The content design was the product. Each card was a self-contained unit of cognitive dissonance. A quote from a church leader, fully sourced, presented without editorial commentary. The design of the card itself did the work. The source citation was prominent, not buried. The visual tone was serious and respectful, not mocking. The red asterisk in the logo was the only signal that this wasn't an official church resource.

The cards covered race, gender, polygamy, sexuality, obedience, discipline, and personal worth. Every one sourced from official publications, general conference talks, or canonized texts. The specificity was the entire strategy. Vague criticism bounces off. Sourced internal contradictions don't.

## The methodology

Religious belief is a load-bearing structure. It holds up identity, community, family relationships, moral framework, afterlife expectations, and daily routine. You can't just remove it. The person needs to build something else before they can let go. Otherwise the whole structure collapses, and the brain knows this. That's why evidence alone doesn't work. The brain rejects evidence that threatens load-bearing beliefs faster than it processes the evidence itself.

Because I came from inside this culture, I knew the first barrier wasn't ignorance. It was the fear that looking too closely was itself a moral failure. The design had to make that fear less expensive to override.

So I designed around this.

### Step 1: Enter through the existing framework

Every piece of content started inside the believer's own worldview. Not challenging it. Using it. Referencing the same texts, the same language, the same values. The person needed to feel understood before they could feel questioned.

This is the same principle behind good onboarding: you meet the user where they are, not where you want them to be. You use their language, their mental model, their existing context.

### Step 2: Introduce contradictions the framework can't resolve

Once inside the framework, I introduced specific, concrete contradictions from within the source material itself. Not external critiques. Internal inconsistencies. Things the believer's own tradition says that contradict other things the tradition says.

The key was specificity. Vague challenges bounce off. Specific, sourced, internal contradictions create genuine cognitive dissonance because the person can't dismiss them as outsider ignorance.

### Step 3: Create space, not conclusions

The content never told anyone what to think. It presented the contradiction and stopped. The design of the space around the content (visual tone, comment moderation, community norms) was built to hold dissonance without resolving it. People needed time to sit with the question before they could find their own answer.

Most persuasion design makes the mistake of closing too fast. Presenting evidence and immediately asking for conversion. That triggers the exact defensive response you're trying to avoid. Leaving space is counterintuitive but it's the only thing that works with load-bearing beliefs.

## The emotional design layer

Beyond the content methodology, the platform design itself was an emotional design exercise. Every visual decision had an emotional intent.

**Typography** was warm and bookish, not clinical or aggressive. The content dealt with serious intellectual material and the type needed to signal "we're thinking together" not "I'm proving you wrong."

**Color** was neutral and warm. The primary palette was dark and archival, like handling original documents. The red accent was used sparingly, only to highlight the specific words that created the contradiction. No cold blue (authority), no aggressive red backgrounds (opposition). The palette said "primary source material" not "attack ad."

**The card format** was designed for sharing, not reading. Each piece of content needed to work as a standalone image on Facebook, Twitter, or in a text message. The citation had to be legible at social media resolution. The source had to be verifiable in under 30 seconds. The design constraint was: someone sees this card, feels dissonance, and can verify the source before the defensive response kicks in.

**Pacing** was intentional. Content released slowly. No firehose of challenges. Each piece had time to breathe, to be discussed, to be absorbed before the next one arrived.

**Defensibility** was structural. The audience's first defense was always going to be *"that's anti-Mormon distortion."* Every artifact was designed so the viewer could verify the source before rejecting the claim. The citation wasn't supporting metadata. It was part of the emotional experience. It gave the viewer a path from "that can't be true" to "I can check this." That path was the whole game.

## Beyond the platform

The project expanded beyond social media. I presented at the 2018 Sunstone Symposium (Session 162), which led to a feature article in Sunstone Magazine: "The Memeing of Mormonism." The methodology got attention not just as advocacy but as a case study in how design and social media can function as persuasion tools.

I also wrote and published the book, [*Stuff You Missed In Sunday School: An Examination of LDS Doctrine, Volume One: Moral Authority*](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0GGNM5Y3W). The book formalized the research into a longer format. It examines the church's historical positions on race, gender, and polygamy using their own documented sources. The same methodology, different medium. Same principle: let the primary sources create the dissonance. Don't editorialize. Don't close.

After four years inside the work, I handed the platform to a team willing to carry the methodology forward. It still runs at [missedinsunday.com](https://missedinsunday.com).

## The transfer

The methodology I built here shows up in everything I design now. Not the religious content. The emotional architecture.

It shows up in how I approach any product where the user resists the thing that would help them: onboarding that asks for vulnerability, health products that surface difficult truths, financial tools that make avoidance visible, AI workflows that challenge existing habits, community products where trust has to be earned before behavior can change. Hiki's consent-first onboarding for neurodivergent users runs on the same principle — enter through the user's existing framework, then introduce the different model.

Meet them where they are. Introduce dissonance from within their own framework. Create space for them to find their own conclusion. Don't close too fast.

That's what this project taught me. And it's not something you learn from a design systems blog post.
