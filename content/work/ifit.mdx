## What this was

2011. Icon Health & Fitness was a hardware company. Treadmills with small screens. Workout videos on a disc. One product surface, 20,000 users, a creative team of three that I was one of.

By 2022, connected fitness platform. Treadmills, bikes, rowers, mobile app, web dashboard. Six million users, $1.7 billion in annual revenue. 300+ people in the iFIT sector alone. The company had renamed itself from Icon Health & Fitness to iFIT because the subscription business had become the business. I ran a 14-person creative team — 12 designers and 2 copywriters — across product, marketing, and social.

I stayed eleven years. My job changed shape constantly. Some years I was a hands-on product designer working on treadmill UI or subscription onboarding. Some years I was a creative director running the team, hiring, reviewing work. Often both in the same week. The pattern that emerged: leadership pulled me onto whatever was high-priority and ambiguous. The NordicTrack checkout was one of those projects.

## The proposal that became the project

2021. I wasn't on the NordicTrack DTC team. But I'd been watching the funnel and saw something specific: seven checkout steps for a $3,797 treadmill, with the iFIT subscription living as its own forced interstitial that made the customer pick a membership tier before they could even configure their machine.

So I made a deck. Not a request. Just the deck.

Current flow: Sales page → Select iFIT Membership → Cart: Shipping → Cart: Options → Cart: Payment → Cart: iFIT → Cart: Confirmation.

Recommended: Sales page → Cart: Shipping → Cart: Payment → Cart: Confirmation.

Three screens disappeared. The "Select iFIT Membership" gate died. The "Cart: Options" and "Cart: iFIT" screens collapsed into the main cart as grouped configuration tiers. The deck opened with one sentence:

> Simplification is the emphasis here, without entirely abandoning marketing. The design lifts liberally from competitor content, leveraging the market research they've already done.

Leadership read it and put me on the project.

## The redesign

Six moves. Each one has a current-vs-recommended slide in the deck.

**Wizard → single-page configurator.** The current cart was a multi-step form. The recommendation turned it into one page: product image on the left, configuration panel on the right. The customer could see the treadmill the whole time they configured it. That sounds obvious. It wasn't happening.

<ImagePlaceholder alt="Recommended cart design: single-page configurator with product, config panel, inline FAQ" caption="One page. Product always visible. Configuration grouped into three decisions. FAQ below the fold, handling objections before they surfaced." brief="The 'Recommended' screen from the Select iFIT Membership slide. NordicTrack page with treadmill image on left. Configuration panel on right grouping Interactive virtual training (iFIT 30 Day / 1 Year tiers), Equipment protection (Manufacturer's / 3 Year Extended / 3 Year Maintenance), Delivery (Threshold / Room of Choice). Price summary reads '$3,797 or $89/mo'. Add to cart button. Chat with a sales expert link. 30-day return policy and free shipping trust signals. FAQ section below." priority="HIGH" style="Product UI, light theme, e-commerce detail page." />

**Integrated iFIT, not bolted-on.** The current flow forced a separate iFIT account-creation interstitial that turned the subscription into its own obstacle. The recommended flow integrated iFIT as a selectable tier inside the main cart. 30-day included. 1-year adds $324 (saves $72). Same decision, a fraction of the friction. This was the explicit ask from leadership: make iFIT feel like a reason to buy the treadmill, not a third-party add-on.

**Inline FAQ as objection handling.** Seven embedded questions — *Is an iFIT Membership required? If I don't renew can I still use my machine? How do I cancel? What's covered under the warranty?* — placed directly below the configurator instead of hidden in a help center. The objections silently killing conversion got answered in the purchase flow itself.

**Financing frontloaded.** Every price in the flow read "$3,797 or $89/mo. 0% APR for 39 mos." together. Not hidden behind a "learn more" link. A $3,797 treadmill read as a $90/month decision.

**Grouped, modular add-on tiers.** Instead of a flat list of upsells, three clean comparisons: Interactive training (30-day / 1-year). Equipment protection (Manufacturer's / 3-Yr Extended / 3-Yr Maintenance). Delivery (Threshold / Room of Choice). Every add-on had explicit pricing. Nothing hidden, nothing sprung at the confirmation step.

**Activation-based billing.** The sharp one. The subscription clock doesn't start when the customer orders. It starts when they plug the treadmill in and turn it on. One design decision solved four problems at once: delivery-delay anxiety, subscription-without-use frustration, activation drop-off, and return rates. This tied to the on-device first-run flow I'd also designed. The web checkout and the treadmill activation are one commercial system, not two.

## The outcome

Contentstack-confirmed conversion lift across revenue, conversion rate, and site performance after the redesign shipped. 44% of iFIT's hardware revenue flowed through the DTC channel. Company revenue that year was $1.7 billion. The design decisions in this funnel moved real money.

## Beyond the checkout

Eleven years at one company means a lot of work that isn't any single screen.

I did the S1 documentation when iFIT pushed for its IPO. Every investor deck. Every page of the public offering materials that went in front of institutional investors deciding whether the company was worth billions. Translating the company's entire story — growth trajectory, competitive position, future vision — into a format that convinces the most skeptical audience there is. The offering didn't close. The SPAC and IPO market collapsed in late 2021, Peloton's stock cratered around the same time. That doesn't change the work. I was trusted at the executive level to shape how the company presented itself to the capital markets.

I built and ran the creative team. Started as one of three designers. By year eleven, 12 designers and 2 copywriters stretched across product, marketing, and social. I didn't inherit the team. I hired every role. The structure decisions mattered more than the headcount. I put a gym on site and gave designers dedicated time to use it, because you can't design workout software you've never sweated through. Set up call shadowing so designers heard real customer frustration firsthand. Ran internal fitness competitions that forced designers to use the product as actual users, not testers. Required real data in every mockup. Placeholder data teaches you to ignore the information layer.

I did the work beyond product UI. Storyboarded TV commercials. Directed photo shoots for the product line. Modeled and rendered 3D animations for an iFIT wearable that went to China for press-check before the line got shelved. Designed packaging for iFIT Nourish, the nutrition spinoff. Shepherded a design-system rebuild with an outside agency as the internal owner driving adoption across surfaces.

Some of this was me hands-on. Some was me concepting and handing off to the team. By year eight, the job was player-coach. I'd do the first round of high-priority work myself, then hand it off. When a project was high-priority and ambiguous, it ended up on my desk.

## What eleven years teaches you

Stay at one company long enough and you stop optimizing for the individual screen. The work that compounded was the proposal that got me onto NordicTrack, the team I hired and trusted, the IPO documentation the exec team signed off on, the design-system adoption I drove after someone else built it. None of that was any single artifact.

The NordicTrack checkout is the piece I point to when someone asks what I've shipped with a number attached. But the real story is that the trust to parachute into it was earned over a decade of being the person leadership called when they didn't want the project to fail.
