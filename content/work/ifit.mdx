## What this was

2011. iFIT was a hardware company. Treadmills with small screens. Workout videos on a disc. One product surface, one screen size, one OS. 20,000 users.

By 2022, connected fitness platform. Treadmills, bikes, rowers, mobile app, web dashboard. Five product surfaces, three operating systems, 6 million users, $1.7 billion in annual revenue. A 20-person creative team I'd built from scratch. The company had done a rebrand, an IPO push, multiple product acquisitions.

I was there for all eleven years. My job changed shape constantly, and the work I'm proudest of isn't any single screen. It's the infrastructure, the revenue impact, and the organizational decisions that compounded over time.

## The infrastructure that made everything else possible

2017. A user reported that the "Start Workout" button on the bike looked different from the same button on the treadmill. Different corner radius. Slightly different blue. Different font weight.

I pulled the thread and found 23 variants of what should have been one button. Across five surfaces, three designers had each built their own version because there was no single source of truth.

I had two paths forward. Build a component library (fast, visible, immediate results) or build a token layer first (slower, invisible for two months, but every decision encoded once and inherited everywhere). I chose tokens. Had to defend that choice to leadership multiple times during those two months of nothing visible changing.

Why: component libraries without shared tokens create a different kind of inconsistency. The button looks the same until someone changes a border radius. Then one surface updates and the other doesn't. Nobody knows which is right. Tokens first means one decision, one update, universal propagation.

<ImagePlaceholder alt="Token architecture diagram showing primitives feeding semantics feeding components across all five surfaces" caption="Primitives feed semantics, semantics feed components. Bottom: component tokens branching to five product surfaces. Arrows flow downward." priority="HIGH" style="System architecture diagram. Three rules governed the system.

**Shared atoms, not shared molecules.** A treadmill screen (22 inches, landscape, viewed while running) and a mobile app (6 inches, portrait, held in one hand) need fundamentally different layouts. I shared the atomic layer (buttons, inputs, type, color, icons) and let each surface compose its own layouts. The atoms were universal. The molecules were local.

**Documentation before production.** Every new component required a one-page spec before it could be used. The spec forced edge-case thinking before production surfaced the edge cases. Every component that shipped without documentation generated a support question from another designer within two weeks.

**Distributed ownership.** Each surface had a lead designer who owned their components. The systems team (me and eventually one other person) owned the token layer and review process. Governance lived in the architecture, not in a queue.

Result: new designers went from needing 2 to 3 weeks to become productive down to 2 to 3 days. The system carried institutional knowledge that used to live in people's heads. That's organizational leverage. One artifact, every hire faster.

## The checkout that processed hundreds of millions

This is the part that matters most to anyone who cares about business impact.

I redesigned the NordicTrack checkout flow. The direct-to-consumer purchase experience on nordictrack.com for hardware that started at $1,000 and went up from there. Treadmills, bikes, rowers. High-ticket, high-friction purchases.

In fiscal year 2021, 44% of iFIT's interactive hardware revenue flowed through DTC channels. The company's total revenue that year was $1.7 billion. That checkout flow was a significant piece of how the company made money.

<ImagePlaceholder alt="NordicTrack checkout flow redesign showing the purchase journey" caption="The checkout redesign. We A/B tested the old checkout against the new. The results, confirmed publicly in a Contentstack case study about the NordicTrack platform, showed immediate improvements in revenue, conversions, and site performance across the board.

The context tells the story: 44% of hardware revenue for a company doing $1.7 billion annually. The design decisions in that checkout (reducing steps, building trust signals for high-ticket purchases, optimizing the mobile-to-desktop handoff that most buyers went through) had direct, measurable financial consequences.

## Beyond product surfaces

Most designers don't create IPO documentation. I did.

When iFIT pushed for its public offering, I created all of it. Every investor deck. The official public offering documentation. The materials that would go in front of institutional investors deciding whether this company was worth billions. This was translating a company's entire story (growth trajectory, competitive position, future vision) into a format that convinces the most skeptical audience there is.

The IPO ultimately didn't close. The whole SPAC and IPO market collapsed in late 2021. Peloton's stock cratered around the same time. That doesn't change the work. I was trusted at the executive level to shape how the company presented itself to the capital markets.

### Building the team

I didn't inherit a team. I built one. Started as the only designer. By the end: 20 people across product design, marketing design, social media, animation, and copywriting.

The structure decisions mattered more than the headcount. I built empathy into the operating model, not the workshop calendar. Put a gym on site and gave designers dedicated time to use it, because you can't design workout software you've never sweated through. Set up call shadowing so designers heard real customer frustration firsthand. Ran internal fitness competitions that forced designers to use the product as actual users, not testers. Required real data in every mockup because placeholder data teaches you to ignore the information layer.

### The profile hub

After leaving iFIT full-time, I came back as a freelancer to redesign the user profile. The brief: users lacked a sense of progress. The app showed workout data but nothing about the experience said "you're getting better." People opened the profile, saw numbers, and closed it.

I turned the profile into a personalized hub. Summary stats with 7-day, 30-day, and 12-month views. Visual data exploration, not just data display. Streaks and achievements that surfaced patterns you wouldn't notice on your own. Workout history that actually worked as a history. Avatar customization with a QR code for photo upload. The whole thing built for both the 22-inch treadmill screen and the 10-inch tablet.

## What eleven years teaches you

The decisions that mattered most weren't visual. They were architectural and organizational. Token-first or component-first. Shared molecules or shared atoms. Centralized ownership or distributed. Document before or after production. Put a gym in the office or assume your designers will figure out empathy on their own.

Each decision compounded over years. The token-first choice I made in 2017 was still paying dividends in 2020, long after I'd stopped thinking about it. The checkout work proved design decisions have direct revenue consequences when the stakes are high enough. The IPO work proved design leadership extends beyond product screens when the trust is there. And the team proved that empathy baked into the operating model outperforms empathy workshops every single time.
