## What this was

2011\. Icon Health & Fitness was a hardware company. Treadmills, ellipticals, gym equipment with small embedded screens. One product surface. I was the only designer in the building.

By 2022, six million users. $1.7 billion in revenue. The company had renamed itself iFIT because the subscription business had become the business. I ran a 14-person creative team across product, marketing, and social. Twelve designers, two copywriters.

I stayed eleven years. My job changed shape constantly. Some years hands-on, working on treadmill UI or onboarding. Some years running the team, hiring, reviewing. Often both in the same week. Leadership pulled me onto whatever was high-priority and ambiguous. The NordicTrack checkout was one of those.

## The proposal that became the project

2021\. I wasn't on the NordicTrack DTC team. But I'd been watching the funnel.

Seven steps to buy a $3,800 treadmill. The iFIT subscription lived as its own forced gate: pick a membership tier before you could even configure your machine.

So I made a deck. Not a request. Just the deck.

Current flow: Sales page → Select iFIT Membership → Cart: Shipping → Cart: Options → Cart: Payment → Cart: iFIT → Cart: Confirmation.

Recommended: Sales page → Cart: Shipping → Cart: Payment → Cart: Confirmation.

Three screens disappeared. The "Select iFIT Membership" gate died. The "Cart: Options" and "Cart: iFIT" screens collapsed into the main cart as grouped configuration tiers. The deck opened with one sentence:

> Simplification is the emphasis here, without entirely abandoning marketing. The design lifts liberally from competitor content, leveraging the market research they've already done.

Leadership read it and put me on the project.

## The redesign

Six moves. Each one had a current-vs-recommended slide in the deck.

**Wizard → single-page configurator.** The current cart was a multi-step form. The recommendation: one page. Product on the left, configuration panel on the right. The customer could see the treadmill the whole time they configured it. That sounds obvious. It wasn't happening.

**Integrated iFIT, not bolted-on.** The current flow forced a separate iFIT account-creation step that turned the subscription into its own obstacle. The recommendation made iFIT a selectable tier inside the main cart. 30 days included. One year added $324 (saved $72). One decision, less friction. This was the explicit ask from leadership: make iFIT feel like a reason to buy the treadmill, not a third-party add-on.

**Inline FAQ as objection handling.** Seven embedded questions placed below the configurator instead of buried in a help center. *Is an iFIT Membership required? If I don't renew, can I still use my machine? How do I cancel? What's covered under the warranty?* The objections silently killing conversion got answered in the purchase flow.

**Financing frontloaded.** Every price read "$3,800 or $89/mo. 0% APR for 39 mos." together. Not behind a "learn more" link. A $3,800 treadmill became a $90/month decision.

**Grouped, modular add-on tiers.** Instead of a flat list of upsells, three clean comparisons. Interactive training (30-day or 1-year). Equipment protection (Manufacturer's, 3-Yr Extended, 3-Yr Maintenance). Delivery (Threshold or Room of Choice). Every add-on had explicit pricing. Nothing hidden. Nothing sprung at the confirmation step.

**Activation-based billing.** The sharp one. The subscription clock doesn't start when the customer orders. It starts when they plug the machine in. One change solved four problems: delivery-delay anxiety, subscription-without-use frustration, activation drop-off, and return rates. The web checkout and the treadmill activation are one commercial system, not two.

## The outcome

The redesign shipped. Contentstack documented the lift after cutover: conversion went up. Revenue went up. Site performance went up. 44% of iFIT's hardware revenue ran through the DTC channel that year. Company revenue: $1.7 billion. This funnel moved real money.

## What checkout earned

The redesign shipped. The numbers came in. Leadership extended the brief.

The same thesis applied to the rest of the funnel. The NordicTrack product detail pages were still running the old template. Generic e-commerce. Spec sheet. iFIT mentioned as one feature among many. That contradicted everything the checkout redesign argued for.

I redesigned the product line PDPs. Switched the brand motif to dark mode. Made a $3,800 treadmill look like a $3,800 treadmill from the moment someone landed on the page. Restructured the page narrative so iFIT membership wasn't another feature; it was the through-line. Directed a full reshoot of the product line for premium photography that matched the price tag. The product page stopped reading as e-commerce and started reading as a premium fitness brand.

## Beyond the checkout

Eleven years at one company means a lot of work that isn't any single screen.

I did the S1 documentation when iFIT pushed for its IPO. Every investor deck. Every page of the public offering materials that went in front of institutional investors deciding whether the company was worth billions. The work was translating the whole company story. Growth, competition, future. Into a format that convinces the most skeptical audience there is.

The offering didn't close. The SPAC and IPO market collapsed in late 2021. Peloton cratered the same year. That doesn't change the work. The exec team handed me the company narrative and trusted me with how it landed.

I built and ran the creative team. I was the only designer at the start. The other two on the team were a PM and a developer. By year eleven, twelve designers and two copywriters across product, marketing, and social. I didn't inherit the team. I hired every role.

The structure decisions mattered more than the headcount. We put a gym on site. Designers got paid, dedicated time to use it. Every hire got a free piece of iFIT-enabled equipment for their home gym. You can't design workout software you've never sweated through. Set up call shadowing so designers heard real customer frustration firsthand. Ran internal fitness competitions that forced designers to use the product as actual users, not testers. Required real data in every mockup. Placeholder data teaches you to ignore the information layer.

I did the work beyond product UI. Storyboarded TV commercials. Modeled 3D animations for an iFIT wearable that went to China for press-check before the line got shelved. Designed packaging for iFIT Nourish, the nutrition spinoff. Ran a design-system rebuild with an outside agency as the internal owner.

Some of this was me hands-on. Some was me concepting and handing off. By year eight, the job was player-coach. First round myself, then hand off. When something high-priority and ambiguous landed, it landed on my desk.

## What eleven years teaches you

Stay at one company long enough and you stop optimizing for the individual screen. None of what compounded was a single artifact. The proposal got me onto NordicTrack. The team I built held the company through scaling. The IPO documents got the exec team in front of the right rooms. The design-system rebuild changed how everyone after me made decisions.

The NordicTrack checkout is the piece I point to when someone asks what I've shipped with a number attached. The real story is the decade of being the person leadership called when they didn't want the project to fail.
