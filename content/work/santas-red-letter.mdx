## The origin

The whole thing started because one of my kids asked if Santa could write them a letter. Not a form letter. A real one. Personal. Something that knew their name, what they'd been doing, specific things only Santa would know.

I said yes. Then I had about six weeks to figure out how to actually make that work and sell it to other parents before Christmas.

## Two months to launch

I designed everything. The brand, the Shopify store, the letter templates, the personalization system, the checkout flow, the packaging, the fulfillment workflow. Two months from idea to taking orders.

The constraint was the calendar. Christmas doesn't move. Every decision had to optimize for one thing: can we ship personalized letters to families before December 25th? There was no time for market research or competitive analysis. The product either worked or it didn't, and I'd know by Christmas morning.

The key discovery was the USPS North Pole Holiday Cancellation Service. Real North Pole postmark on a real red envelope with a real wax seal. The letter itself was printed on parchment with Santa's gold foil signature. $9.95 per letter, free shipping. A Certificate of Nice came with every Red Letter. The whole thing was designed to survive the skepticism of a seven-year-old.

## Seven years of iteration

The first year was proof of concept. It worked. Families loved it. Kids lost their minds. Parents cried. That's a product.

Year two I fixed everything that broke in year one. The personalization system got smarter. The letter designs got better. The fulfillment process stopped being me at a kitchen table at 2am.

By year three the business was real enough to expand. I launched **Santa's Black Coal**, a gag gift line for the "naughty list" crowd. Real coal in a black gift box, branded "Mined in the North Pole." Then **White Elephant**, a holiday gift brand with kraft-paper packaging and a clean elephant logo. Three product lines under one brand umbrella, each targeting a different segment of the holiday gift market.

The brand system had to flex across three completely different emotional registers. Red Letter was warm, personal, magic-preserving. Black Coal was dark humor, gag gift energy, the kind of thing you send anonymously to a coworker. White Elephant was gift exchange territory, playful and neutral. Same design system. Three distinct personalities. That's a brand architecture problem most designers only get to solve at agency scale.

Seven years of iteration taught me things about product design that no amount of working inside someone else's company could teach. When the fulfillment cost comes out of your pocket, you learn to simplify the product. When customer service emails come to your inbox, you learn what actually matters to people. When the revenue is yours, you care about conversion in a way that's impossible to fake.

## Toys for Tots

$1 from every order went to Toys for Tots. That partnership ran for over a decade. We did shopping trips with the kids, filled carts with toys, documented the whole thing. It wasn't a marketing play. The product was already about making kids feel seen. Connecting with a charity that puts toys in the hands of kids who wouldn't otherwise get them was the obvious next step once the business could support it.

## The press

The press came without a publicist. Every hit was earned.

HuffPost ran a feature calling it a startup that had "finally solved Santa's mail problem." Shopify featured it as a success story on how to build a seasonal e-commerce business. Cool Mom Picks recommended it. E&E News (POLITICO) wrote about the Black Coal line under the headline "The coal of choice for Santa." KUTV (the CBS2 affiliate in Utah) ran a television segment. Crystal went on air and showed the product.

None of this was planned. The product was designed well enough and priced right enough that press found it on its own. That's the best kind of marketing validation: journalists choosing to cover your product because it's actually interesting.

## The exit

After seven years I sold the business. Not because it wasn't working. Because I'd built the thing I set out to build, learned what I needed to learn, and was ready to focus on other work.

The design skills transfer directly. Brand systems that flex across sub-brands. E-commerce checkout optimization when the revenue is literally yours. Packaging design. Fulfillment workflow design. Personalization systems. Charity partnerships. Press strategy without a budget.

But the real transfer is the founder mindset. When you've shipped product where failure means your own money and your kid's Christmas, you bring a different kind of urgency to every project after. Not frantic. Just real. The stakes aren't abstract anymore.

## What this proves

I can build things from zero. Not prototypes. Not concepts. Real products that real people pay for, use, and remember. Products that get press coverage without a marketing budget. Products I iterated on for seven years and then sold.

Most designer portfolios don't have this. That's the point.
